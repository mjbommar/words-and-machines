# SPIRIT-TEMPLATE — The Soul Document

Every book gets a `docs/SPIRIT.md`. The style guides tell you *how* to write; SPIRIT
tells you *why* — not the facts, but the feeling. It is the highest-priority doc for
drafting agents: when a passage is technically correct but dead, the diagnosis is almost
always "it doesn't touch a thread."¹

**How to use this template:**
1. Copy everything below the divider into `docs/SPIRIT.md` and fill in the brackets.
2. Write it *before* drafting; revise it only deliberately (it's the book's constitution).
3. Keep it short — one sitting to read. Facts live in research/; this is the soul.
4. Every drafting and review prompt should include "read SPIRIT.md first."

Rules of thumb learned from use:
- **Two or three emotional threads, no more.** Each thread is a feeling with a mechanism.
  Every paragraph in the book should touch at least one.
- **The thesis is one sentence** and it should be quotable. If you can't say it in one
  breath, you don't have it yet.
- **Give chapters a recurring closing move** (a coda) and require its wording to vary —
  benediction, not boilerplate.
- **Name the mentor authors "for feel, not formula."**

## Filled mini-example (condensed from a real SPIRIT.md)

> **Title's promise** — *Rough Consensus: How Engineers, Hackers, and Spies Built the
> Internet.* Characters (real people, not abstractions), action ("built" is generative),
> tension (the main title hints at fragility; the subtitle is constructive — the book
> reveals what the fragility cost).
>
> **Thesis** — *The same thing that made the internet vulnerable is what made it
> possible. And that thing is still running.*
>
> **Thread 1: The Contrast (grief).** What it was → what it became. Four nodes became
> five billion users; trusted colleagues became anonymous strangers; the assumptions that
> made sense became the vulnerabilities exploited. When writing about origins, let the
> reader feel what was lost — don't romanticize, but honor the simplicity.
>
> **Thread 2: The Miracle (awe).** It still works, every day, despite everything. No one
> planned it; no one controls it; it holds together through patches on patches and good
> faith mostly honored. When writing about the present, let the reader feel the miracle —
> not naive optimism, genuine wonder.
>
> **Journey** — curiosity with vague anxiety → understanding with increasing unease →
> awe and fragile hope ("it's held together by faith and patches, and I'm part of it,
> right now, using it").
>
> **Coda** — every chapter ends the same move: yes, the assumption was violated; yes,
> people were harmed; and yet — the protocol still carries traffic today. Vary the
> benediction: "And still, it runs." / "The mail still arrives." / "The handshake
> completes, billions of times per second."
>
> **A distinctive move** — the reader is *inside* the system: the protocols described are
> carrying the words they're reading. Make it explicit at key moments ("Right now, as you
> read this...").
>
> **Mentors** — Hafner (intimate origins), Kidder (engineers as people), Gleick (wonder
> at vast systems), Stoll and Zetter (breach stories), McPhee (structure).
