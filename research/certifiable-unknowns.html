Certifiable Unknowns

Open problems where a machine could produce genuinely new mathematics and hand back an object small enough to check. Ranked against what this stack measurably does today — not what its documentation says it does.

01

The asymmetry is the whole map

Three independent sweeps — combinatorics, engineering, optimization — converged on one structural fact without being told to look for it. In essentially every open problem worth attacking, the two directions cost wildly different amounts to verify.

Improvement / witness

Milliseconds

A better circuit, a shorter tour, a valid colouring, a rank‑22 scheme. You replay the object. No solver in the loop, no trust required.

Optimality / nonexistence

Petabytes, or nothing at all

Schur 5 was a 2 PB proof. Boolean Pythagorean triples, 200 TB. For most published lower bounds, no certificate format exists in the first place.

That asymmetry decides which problems are reachable. It also has exactly three escape hatches — shapes where the negative answer is cheap to check too. Two separate sweeps arrived at the same three, which is the most reusable thing either produced:

  1. Univariate nonnegativity on an interval. Sum‑of‑squares is complete here via Markov–Lukács — no degree bound to guess, no relaxation gap. The certificate is exact rational.
  2. SDP infeasibility with a rational dual ray. One small rational matrix and one inner product. Sometimes smaller than the positive certificate.
  3. Small pure‑SAT instances. DRAT falls straight out of a proof‑producing CDCL core for free — which this repository already has.
02

What this stack has already done

Worth stating plainly, because it sets the bar and it is easy to forget: this project has already produced new mathematics. The claim ledger carries 21 entries marked novelty: new — 18 off‑diagonal Schur numbers and 3 Rado numbers — of which two, R₄(5(x−y)=3z) = 625 and R₄(5(x−y)=4z) = 741, carry external_status: open, meaning no published value existed.

The route was SAT search → self‑produced DRAT → independent backward check → witness replay. Three numbers from that run govern everything below.

Certificate production is fast. Certificate checking and elaboration is what fails to terminate — the fp16 monotonicity miter is worse in ratio, 24 seconds of search against three hours of checking. So “can we solve it” and “can we ship a checkable proof of it” have different answers, and the second one binds.

B  ·  Gap‑of‑one instances in applied cryptography

Best new-result odds

These are published as intervals: UNSAT proven at k−1, SAT found at k. Each is one hard call from closed, at a size this stack handles, with a public encoding. And the authors state that prior published optimality results “may cause potentially incorrect results” and had to be re‑verified — so a DRAT‑certified answer would be the first ever produced for an S‑box, a soundness upgrade rather than a speed one.

RF₂(P₆) is the closest fit: roughly 7k variables and 50k clauses, hard because of symmetry rather than size, and it underpins GHASH — which runs in every TLS stack and NIC offload on earth. The precedent to imitate is Wang's 2026 bound R(⟨3,3,3⟩) ≥ 20 over F₂: forty minutes to search on a laptop, verifies in seconds, independently re‑checked by a third party who wrote their own checker in a different language. That is this project's thesis, already demonstrated in the target domain.

C  ·  Certify results nobody has ever certified

Zero new mathematics

The optimization sweep found an unoccupied niche that is this project's exact thesis. Primal results across the whole field are checked rigorously — MIPLIB runs an exact GMP rational checker on every submitted solution. Dual results are checked by nobody.

The state‑of‑the‑art graph‑colouring lower bounds rest on a custom CaDiCaL propagator with no proof logging of any kind. No machine‑checkable certificate exists for any job‑shop instance — open or closed. One nurse‑rostering benchmark says it in plain text: “It is not possible to verify lower bounds.” And a 2026 paper published a 0.0000% optimality gap that is refuted by a better feasible solution which had been sitting on MIPLIB for nine months.

Concrete targets, in rising order of effort: exact‑rational PSD certificates for the Krpan–Povh 2026 Lovász‑theta clique bounds (turning published numbers into checkable objects); proof‑logging existing chromatic lower bounds via VeriPB; the first certificate for a closed job‑shop instance such as abz07 = 656.

05

Do not trust the registries

A finding that arrived sideways and matters more than any single problem. The optimization sweep checked update dates on every benchmark library it touched. Six of eight are unmaintained, offline, or publish no status at all.

  • 2002DIMACS COLOR still marks queen10_10 unknown — settled in 2004
  • 2011QAPLIB's last What's‑New entry; every gap percentage quoted from it is 15 years old
  • 2015SteinLib still marks instances open that were closed by 2020
  • 2020The best public Steiner results table, six years stale
  • —BPPLIB publishes no optimality status; CVRPLIB was entirely offline all session

An “open instance” sourced from any of these is a claim about 2011–2020, not about 2026. This is the same pathology this repository already worries about at close range — a ledger nobody is running — arriving from a sixth direction. It is also a warning about the survey you are reading: verify currency before committing a roadmap to any single entry above.

One inversion proves the point. Classical one‑dimensional bin packing has no named open instance as of April 2026. The AI and ANI classes were designed to be hard, defeated MIP solvers for a decade, and then fell — not to a bigger solver, but to a structural theorem showing the restricted problem is not strongly NP‑hard, followed by a polynomial‑time algorithm. A benchmark can measure the wrong thing for ten years.

06

What I would actually pick

Lane B, starting with RF₂(P₆), then the three S‑box intervals.

It is the only lane where all four criteria hold at once. The novelty is real and externally confirmed — these are published intervals, not unformalized folklore. The instances are small enough that this stack's proof‑producing core is the right tool rather than a liability. Both directions carry certificates, and the negative one is DRAT, which the repository already emits and independently checks. And the audience is immediate: Ascon is the NIST lightweight standard, Keccak is FIPS 202, and AND count is literally the cost metric for every MPC and zero‑knowledge evaluation.

Run Lane A in parallel as throughput — it needs no new machinery and each cell is a genuine result. Treat Lane C as the strategic bet: it needs no new mathematics, and it is the one place where this project's actual thesis has no competitor.
