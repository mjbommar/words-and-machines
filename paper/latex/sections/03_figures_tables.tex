\subsection{A vacuous certificate}
\label{sec:vacuous}

The negative control failed on the first row. The unary five-family one-step formula is refuted by unit propagation alone: following the consequences of its clauses assigns all 2,663 variables and conflicts, with no search. For such a formula the empty clause is itself RUP from the formula, so a backward checker asked whether the final empty clause is justified answers yes---for \emph{any} proof file whose last step is the empty clause. We confirmed this three ways: the shipped proof is accepted with its last two bytes removed, with its last fifty lines removed, and with its first half deleted; an empty proof and a garbage proof are correctly rejected (they lack the empty clause). The other two formulas reach a propagation fixpoint without conflict; their proofs are load-bearing and their truncation controls fail as they should.

\begin{quote}
\emph{If a CNF conflicts under unit propagation, every DRAT file ending in the empty clause is accepted by a backward checker. The certificate is then the formula; the proof file is decoration.}
\end{quote}

Nothing here makes the theorem false---the formula is unsatisfiable and one instruction does not suffice. What changes is the honest description of the evidence: for that object the load-bearing certificate is the formula and a one-line propagation check, not the 957,982-byte file that was shipped and accepted. We regard this as the most useful result in the paper. It is the compiler-side instance of a rule the SAT community learned long ago---a checker that cannot fail is worse than none---moved one step upstream: \emph{a proof that cannot be wrong is worse than no proof, because it reads as evidence.} We now run the propagation test on every formula before believing a small certificate, and record the outcome on the object.

\subsection{The searches a ratified ISA was designed on}
\label{sec:isa}

RISC-V's scalar-crypto repository tabulates, for all 256 three-input Boolean functions, the minimum instruction count with and without Zbkb's \code{andn}/\code{orn}/\code{xnor}, and uses the table to conclude that NOR and NAND instructions would \enquote{not really} help. Its only stated evidence is \enquote{our exhaustive search}: no code, no data, two commits on one day in 2020. We re-implemented a complete breadth-first search over sets of held truth tables and reproduced all 512 entries. We also found what the document does not state: for SHA-2's \code{Ch} and \code{Maj}, the two functions it uses as motivation, the minimum is 3 and 4 in \emph{both} instruction sets---Zbkb saves no instructions there. Two independent uncertified searches now agree; that is still not a proof, and certifying the table is 256 small refutations totalling under a gigabyte.

The Bitmanip draft's permutation-reachability table (Table 2.2) and its claim that all 24 byte permutations of a 32-bit word need at most three of \code{ror}, \code{grev}, \code{shfl} cite a brute-force enumeration whose URL returns 404. We reproduce both exactly: the reachability counts $1, 62, 864, 4640, 23312, \dots, 1048576$ for rotate and generalized-reverse (11/11 entries) and $1, 85, 3030, 78659, 2002167$ with shuffle (5/5 through depth four), and the per-permutation minima the draft omits (6 at length one, 9 at two, 8 at three). The same draft asserts, with no proof, that \enquote{at least 7 instructions are needed to pack a 5:6:5 RGB value}---the only bare lower bound we found in any ISA specification. It is a week's work to decide.
